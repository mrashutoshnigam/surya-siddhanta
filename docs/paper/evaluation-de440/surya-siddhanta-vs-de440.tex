\author{Ashutosh Nigam,  Dr. Dinesh Mohan Joshi}
\date{}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
The \SuSi{} is an influential astronomical text of classical India and its methods were used for panchang-making for many centuries. We implement its planetary theory in Python, using Ebenezer Burgess's English translation \citep{burgess1860} and Mahavir Prasad Shrivastava's Hindi book, \textit{Surya Siddhanta: Vigyan Bhashya} \citep{shrivastava}, as the two textual sources for the implementation. No modern ephemeris provides positions to the Surya Siddhanta calculation. We compare the resulting tropical longitudes of the Sun, Moon, Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn and the lunar node (Rahu) with JPL DE440 at 40,176 ten-day epochs from 1550 to 2649 CE. This window lies within DE440's coverage. Historical epochs evaluate the adopted uncorrected implementation; future epochs show its extrapolation.

We examine mean-motion drift, the residual structure after detrending, the trepidation model of precession (ayanamsha), and the conversion from Ujjain local mean time to Terrestrial Time with \dT{}. Through 2249 CE the per-century RMS longitude error is 1.14 to 1.93 degrees for the Sun, 1.69 to 2.29 for the Moon, and 1.88 to 9.45 for the five star-planets. Saturn has the largest fitted drift in the precession-corrected diagnostic ($-29.5$ arcseconds per Julian year), and Venus the largest detrended RMS. The trepidation contribution rises from $+1.15$ to $+1.92$ degrees before reversing in 2299 CE. By the final epoch it is $-8.25$ degrees, and the last-century RMS for each body from the Sun through Mars exceeds 11 degrees. These results quantify this implementation and comparison convention; they do not directly measure historical panchang practice or eclipse timing.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The \SuSi{} is a Sanskrit astronomical treatise. Its received text dates to somewhere between the fifth and tenth centuries CE, and its parameters are set to an epoch near 499 CE, the year its precession model puts the vernal equinox at the start of sidereal Aries. Across fourteen chapters it gives complete procedures for mean and true planetary positions, eclipses, conjunctions, heliacal risings, the lunar crescent and the calendar. Ebenezer Burgess's English translation of 1860 opened the text to historians of science, and Burgess describes it as still the standard authority of Indian panchang-makers (\textit{panchangkartas}) in his time. It has been studied widely as a document. It has much less often been \emph{run} and measured.

Related computational work includes \citet{santhoju2025}, who implemented location-specific Surya Siddhanta nirayana planetary positions and compared them with Stellarium. Their model includes modifications such as fractional ahargana. Historical and mathematical studies also analyse Indian planetary algorithms and later parameter corrections \citep{pingree1978,duke2005,plofker2009}. The contribution here is a specified 1,100-year DE440 comparison of an uncorrected SS implementation, with explicit frame and time conventions and reproducible statistical diagnostics. We do not claim to be the first to evaluate the Surya Siddhanta computationally.

The question addressed here is narrow. At the sampled dates between 1550 and 2649~CE, how far do the longitudes produced by this implementation depart from the DE440 reference, and which model choices may explain that departure? A historical interpretation would require further evidence about the parameters and procedures that particular panchang-makers actually used. At future epochs, the comparison exposes the consequences of the chosen constants, including the reversal of the trepidation model in 2299~CE.

Section~\ref{sec:impl} describes the implementation and the numerical choices that affect the evaluated longitude path of Chapters~1 and~2. The DE440 comparison is defined in Section~\ref{sec:method}, and Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the errors by body and century together with the fitted secular and long-period components. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses how the model should be interpreted and where it is limited, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes. Section~\ref{sec:availability} states where the code and data are available, and Appendix~\ref{app:verses} maps each evaluated quantity to its corresponding verses and code.

\section{Background}

\subsection{The \textit{Surya Siddhanta} planetary model}

The Surya Siddhanta model is geocentric and eccentric-epicyclic.

Each body starts from a mean longitude (Ch.~1, v.~29--34, 53). This is its number of revolutions in a mahayuga of 4,320,000 years multiplied by the elapsed fraction of the yuga, where the elapsed time is counted in civil days (\textit{ahargana}) from the Kali epoch: midnight of 17/18 February 3102 BCE on the Ujjain meridian. Table~\ref{tab:mean} lists the revolution counts. For Mercury and Venus the mean planet is the mean Sun, and the listed revolutions belong to the \textit{sighrocca}, which corresponds to the planet's heliocentric mean longitude.

The manda correction (Ch.~2, v.~29--45) is the equation of centre. It comes from an epicycle whose circumference is given as a number of degrees of the deferent (the main orbit), and the epicycle itself shrinks or grows with the anomaly (v.~34--38). In place of trigonometric functions the text uses the sine table of Ch.~2 v.~15--27, 24 entries at radius 3438.

The five star-planets also get a sighra correction (Ch.~2, v.~39--45), from a second epicycle centred on the mean planet. Its high point, the \textit{sighrocca}, is the mean Sun for Mars, Jupiter and Saturn, and has its own mean motion for Mercury and Venus. The text applies the two corrections in four steps: half sighra, half manda, full manda, full sighra.

Rahu, the ascending node, has only a mean retrograde motion (Ch.~1, v.~29--34). The text gives it no equation.

Precession (Ch.~3, v.~9--12) is a \emph{trepidation}: an oscillation of amplitude 27 degrees and period 7,200 years, zero in 499 CE. The ayanamsha grows at 54 arcseconds per year until it reaches 27 degrees in 2299 CE, and then decreases. Every Surya Siddhanta longitude is sidereal, and adding the ayanamsha gives the text's tropical longitude.

The Surya Siddhanta has no lunar evection, variation or annual equation. Its Moon has a single manda epicycle.

The node's motion is retrograde, so its positive difference means the Surya Siddhanta node moves backward 9.5 arcseconds per year \emph{more slowly} than the true one. The node is compared with its sidereal rate, as every other row is, not its tropical rate.

The Surya Siddhanta sidereal year is 365.258756 days, against the modern 365.256363, too long by 0.002393 days (3.4 minutes) each year. Every mean longitude tied to the Sun therefore falls behind by 8.5 arcseconds a year, which comes to 2.6 degrees across the 1,100-year window.

\subsection{JPL DE440}

DE440 \citep{park2021} is a numerically integrated ephemeris fitted to spacecraft ranging, lunar laser ranging and astrometric observations. The local kernel spans JD 2287184.5 to 2688976.5, corresponding to 1549-12-31 through 2650-01-25 in the proleptic Gregorian calendar. DE441 provides a longer integration for other time windows. We adopt DE440 as a modern reference at the degree-scale resolution of this study. Its agreement with observations and with other ephemerides does not establish a uniform subarcsecond bound on absolute error across all bodies and the full 1,100-year interval, and its uncertainty grows outside the period covered by observations.

\subsection{Why the frame and the clock matter}
\label{sec:frame-clock}

What counts as ``error'' in this comparison depends on two choices.

The first is the reference frame. Surya Siddhanta longitudes are sidereal, whereas the ICRS vectors of DE440 can be transformed to the mean ecliptic and equinox of date. In the primary comparison, we add the Surya Siddhanta ayanamsha to the sidereal longitude, so the comparison includes the effect of the trepidation model. This is an analytical choice. It does not imply that all traditional panchangs reported tropical longitudes. A second diagnostic subtracts the difference between the Surya Siddhanta ayanamsha and IAU 2006 general precession, and we refer to the result as the \emph{precession-corrected residual}. This residual reflects a scalar correction in longitude rather than a complete rotation into a fixed ecliptic plane, because the ecliptic pole also moves over time.

The second is the clock. The Surya Siddhanta counts from midnight on the Ujjain meridian (75.79 degrees east) in local mean time. DE440 runs on Barycentric Dynamical Time, which for our purposes is Terrestrial Time (TT). Two things separate them. One is the fixed longitude offset of 5.05 hours. The other is \dT{} (TT minus Universal Time), which was about 152\,s in 1550, fell to a minimum of about $-6$\,s around 1893, stood at 64\,s in 2000, and is extrapolated to 2,184\,s by 2649 \citep{espenak2006}. The Moon moves 0.55 degrees an hour, so the longitude offset alone amounts to 2.8 degrees of lunar longitude and \dT{} up to another 0.33. We run the comparison both ways, once without correction, taking the Surya Siddhanta Julian Day as TT, and once with the correction, and report both.

\section{The implementation}
\label{sec:impl}

\subsection{Design}

The library (\file{suryasiddhanta}, Python $\geq$3.9 in package metadata, NumPy as its core dependency) has one module for each chapter of the text. Verse references occur in docstrings and comments, but the evaluated call path is specifically \path{ch01_mean_motions.mean_longitude}, \path{ch02_true_places.true_longitude_full}, and \path{ch02_true_places.ayanamsha}. Calendar conversion uses the Julian calendar for dates before 1582-10-15 and the Gregorian calendar from that date onwards. Civil years are grouped using these calendar dates. The regression uses a separate, uniform time coordinate.

The Surya Siddhanta longitude path does not use a modern ephemeris. It applies the adopted mean-motion constants and the four-step sighra/manda procedure without bija corrections. The function \file{jya()} interpolates the 24-entry sine table at radius 3438, whereas the correction routines use modern inverse-sine and square-root arithmetic, including a derived cosine. The implementation therefore combines historically tabulated sines with modern numerical operations and does not reproduce historical table arithmetic at every step. The true-longitude entry point rounds to 0.0001 degrees (0.36 arcsec). Because of these choices, the discrepancies reported below cannot simply be assumed to arise from the text alone.

\subsection{Verification against worked examples}

The repository includes chapter audits and worked-example tests based on Mahavir Prasad Shrivastava's Hindi book, \textit{Surya Siddhanta: Vigyan Bhashya} \citep{shrivastava}. We consulted this book through OCR transcripts, which systematically misread the Devanagari numeral nine in its period typeface as six. All numerical constants on the evaluated path follow Burgess, and each worked-example value used in a test was checked either arithmetically or against the scan. During development, we corrected the manda-kendra sign convention and the treatment of planetary latitude, lambana and valana. In the revalidation carried out for this paper, all 263 focused tests for Chapters~1 and~2 passed. We also independently reconstructed all 642,816 original residual values against the local DE440 kernel and found agreement to within 0.00000055 ($5.5\times10^{-7}$~degrees) degrees. These checks establish that the implementation is internally consistent. They do not establish complete historical or astronomical correctness. The recent corrections to Chapters~3--14 do not affect this longitude comparison.

\subsection{Limitations of the implementation that bound this study}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The lunar apparent diameter is scaled by the ratio of true to mean daily lunar motion, computed from centred one-day differences. The solar and shadow diameter helpers retain fixed mean values. Full asakrit eclipse iteration is not implemented, and eclipses are not evaluated in this study.
  \item The implementation interprets the star coordinates of Chapter~8 as ecliptic and those of Chapter~13 as polar. Neither is used in this study.
  \item The text gives no equation for the lunar node, so the implementation returns its mean longitude.
\end{itemize}

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Choice of window}
\label{sec:window}

The coverage of the reference ephemeris sets the window of 1550--2649~CE. We considered shifting it but kept it as it is, for four reasons.

First, the chosen civil-year window lies within the local DE440 kernel. Julian 1550-01-01 is JD 2287195.5, eleven days after the kernel begins, and Gregorian 2649-12-25 is JD 2688945.5, 31 days before it ends. These margins accommodate the reference time shift, which moves an epoch earlier by at most approximately 0.21 days. As a result, the selected window is slightly shorter than the full coverage of the kernel.

Second, a much earlier window would require a longer reference ephemeris such as DE441. DE441 omits the lunar core-mantle damping used in DE440 to permit a much longer backward integration. An extension would require checking the overlap and recomputing the results; we do not assume that every displayed digit would remain unchanged. The present study continues to use the available approximately 114\,MiB DE440 kernel.

Third, the historical part of the window benefits from progressively tighter constraints on \dT{}, first from telescopic observations and later from atomic time. Values before this period and extrapolations into the future are less reliably known. This consideration supports the use of the historical interval, but it does not make the entire window equally well determined.

Fourth, the window contains both historical epochs, when the text was in use, and future epochs, which expose the consequences of its parameters. The future epochs include the reversal of the implemented trepidation near 2299~CE. Both intervals evaluate the same uncorrected model. The historical interval alone does not show that these exact parameters were used in practice.

This choice has a cost. The Surya Siddhanta mean motions were set near 499~CE, and their errors grow with distance from that epoch. A window that begins about a thousand years later therefore examines the text after its secular errors have already accumulated (Table~\ref{tab:decomp}, offset column), rather than in the period when it was most accurate.

\subsection{Grid}
\label{sec:grid}

The grid extends from 1550-01-01 in the Julian calendar to 2649-12-25 in the Gregorian calendar, the last ten-day step before 2649-12-31, and contains 40,176 epochs. This sampling does not resolve the approximately 14.8-day variation in lunar longitude. Any harmonic analysis of the resulting aliased terms would therefore require an explicit design matrix and checks on its conditioning, and we do not claim to have separated every lunar term. Epochs are assigned to century groups by civil year, giving eleven groups from 1550--1649 to 2550--2649. Twelve epochs had been placed in the wrong group by the earlier decimal-year labels, and the tables reported here use the corrected groups. For provenance, the regeneration driver records a hash of the little-endian float64 JD array.

\subsection{\textit{Surya Siddhanta} side}

For each epoch and body, we compute the sidereal true longitude and add the ayanamsha of Chapter~3 to obtain the tropical longitude. For Rahu, the implementation uses the longitude of the mean ascending node. The regeneration script (Section~\ref{sec:availability}) recomputes these quantities directly from the chapter functions.

\subsection{Reference side}
\label{sec:reference}

For each epoch, we take the geocentric geometric vectors from DE440 and rotate them from the ICRS to the mean ecliptic and equinox of date using the IAU 2006 formulation \citep{capitaine2003,hilton2006}, which is equivalent to \path{erfa.ecm06}. The regeneration implementation includes frame bias. For Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn, we use the vectors of the corresponding planetary-system barycentres. For the Sun and Moon, we use their kernel vectors relative to Earth. Light-time, aberration and nutation are omitted because the comparison targets geometric longitude in the mean frame. For Rahu, we rotate the geocentric position and velocity of the Moon into this frame, take their cross product and compute the ascending node of the resulting osculating orbital plane. This reference node oscillates about the mean node with a period of roughly 173 days. The mean-node model of the Surya Siddhanta does not contain this oscillation.

We use two versions of the reference time scale:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Uncorrected time} (labelled \file{naive} in the data files and plots): $\mathrm{TT} = \mathrm{JD}$, where JD is the \SuSi{} Julian Day.
  \item \textbf{\dT{} corrected} (labelled \file{deltat}):
  \[
    \mathrm{TT} = \mathrm{JD} - \frac{75.7873}{360} + \frac{\Delta T(\mathrm{JD})}{86400},
  \]
  with \dT{} taken from the piecewise polynomials of \citet{espenak2006}. Before 1600, these polynomials fit the values of \citet{morrison2004}. From 1600 to 2005, they follow the telescopic and modern observational record. After 2005, they are an extrapolation that ends in the long-term parabola $-20 + 32u^2$, where $u$ is measured in centuries from 1820.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

The residual is the Surya Siddhanta tropical longitude minus the DE440 longitude of date, wrapped to $[-180, 180)$. For each civil century, we report the sample count, the mean, the RMS and the signed residual of largest absolute value. The precession-corrected diagnostic subtracts from this residual the difference between the Surya Siddhanta ayanamsha and the accumulated IAU 2006 general precession. Its linear fit uses $x = (\mathrm{JD} - \mathrm{JD}_{2100\text{-}01\text{-}01})/365.25$, so slopes are expressed per Julian year and offsets refer to Gregorian 2100-01-01. The RMS after detrending contains periodic structure as well as any nonlinear secular remainder, so it does not measure epicycle error in isolation. We do not fit the tropical residual with a single trend, because the slope of its frame contribution changes by about 108 arcseconds per year at the trepidation reversal, from approximately $+3.7$ to $-104.4$ arcseconds per year.

\subsection{Reproducibility}

All results were computed from the local DE440 kernel (SHA-256 \texttt{a4ce9bf9\allowbreak b3282bec\allowbreak c9f4b2ac\allowbreak 3cebe03a\allowbreak 2ae75999\allowbreak 81aabd72\allowbreak 65fd8482\allowbreak fff7c4b5}) on the ten-day grid of Section~\ref{sec:grid}, whose JD array has SHA-256 \texttt{8d2d5d7c\allowbreak bda66baf\allowbreak 8b966aa3\allowbreak 8f0fb226\allowbreak 381306cf\allowbreak 842efc38\allowbreak 2953a973\allowbreak 315d235b}. The environment was Python 3.14.6, NumPy 2.5.4 and jplephem 2.24 on Linux. A rerun on 2026-10-13 reproduced every cell of Tables~\ref{tab:rms}--\ref{tab:aya} and every diagnostic quoted in Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:conclusion}; Figures~\ref{fig:sunmoon}--\ref{fig:aya} and the CSV files are the outputs of that run. The regeneration script records package versions, source hashes and output hashes with each saved run (Section~\ref{sec:availability}).

It does not, however, uniquely apportion the fitted slope among mean motion, reference-frame convention, time conversion and unresolved long-period terms.

In this geocentric diagnostic, Mercury and Venus do not show the drift of their sighrocca. Their fitted drifts ($-8.58$ and $-8.51$) agree with that of the Sun ($-8.57$) to within 0.1 arcseconds per Julian year. This agreement is consistent with the Surya Siddhanta treatment in which the mean planet coincides with the mean Sun and the sighrocca produces a bounded geocentric swing. The effect of the sighrocca can therefore remain in the detrended structure instead of appearing as the secular slope of the heliocentric mean element.

To test sensitivity to a long-period component, we jointly fit an intercept, a linear trend and sine/cosine terms with a fixed period of 883 Julian years:
\[
  r = a + b\,x + A\sin(2\pi x/883) + B\cos(2\pi x/883),
\]

Jupiter's fitted drift becomes $+7.144$ arcseconds/year with amplitude 18.463 arcminutes; Saturn's becomes $-27.861$ arcseconds/year with amplitude 44.208 arcminutes. This is a sensitivity calculation motivated by the Jupiter--Saturn great inequality. Over only about 1.25 cycles, the harmonic and trend are correlated, so the fit does not uniquely identify that physical perturbation or explain the remaining difference. Earlier coefficients and the additional anomaly/synodic fit are omitted because their generating method was not reproducible.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Where the error comes from, body by body}
\label{sec:bodies}

The drift, offset and detrended RMS below come from Table~\ref{tab:decomp}; century statistics are computed by the regeneration script (Section~\ref{sec:availability}). Model interpretations are distinguished from measured diagnostics. Detailed lunar harmonic amplitudes and anomaly/elongation-bin estimates from the earlier draft are omitted because their generating scripts, arguments and bin definitions were unavailable.

\paragraph{Sun.} The measured precession-corrected drift is $-8.57$ arcseconds/year, close to the mean-motion prediction of $-8.5$. Before the trepidation reversal, the frame contribution partially cancels this lag: the tropical century means change from $-1.12$ degrees in 1550--1649 to $-1.92$ in 2150--2249. The final-century mean is $-11.50$ degrees. The residual after removing the linear trend is 0.198 degrees RMS. A mismatch in the manda equation is a likely contributor; quantifying individual annual harmonics requires a separately specified fit.

\paragraph{Moon.} The fitted drift is $-8.85$ arcseconds/year, compared with the approximate mean-motion prediction of $-8.0$. \dT{} and long-period structure can contribute to this difference, but this regression does not establish an exact division of causes. The detrended RMS is 1.456 degrees. The single Surya Siddhanta manda epicycle omits the separate evection, variation and annual-equation terms of modern lunar theory. This provides a physical reason to expect periodic differences, without establishing their individual fitted amplitudes. The time-scale conversion reduces the whole-window lunar RMS by 2.06 degrees.

\paragraph{Mercury.} The fitted drift ($-8.58$ arcseconds/year) closely follows the mean Sun. The Surya Siddhanta mean Mercury is the mean Sun, while its sighrocca causes the bounded geocentric swing. The detrended RMS is 2.597 degrees, decreasing from 3.10 in the first civil century to 2.13 in the last. Sighrocca phase and epicycle geometry can contribute to that structure; linking it precisely to perihelion or to an individual harmonic requires additional diagnostics.

\paragraph{Venus.} The fitted drift ($-8.51$ arcseconds/year) also follows the mean Sun. Venus has the largest detrended RMS, 7.205 degrees, increasing from 4.00 in the first civil century to 9.87 in the last. About 10.04\% of sampled epochs have absolute detrended errors above ten degrees, and the largest sampled tropical error is 48.94 degrees. Near inferior conjunction, small geocentric distance can amplify a heliocentric position error. However, small elongation also occurs near superior conjunction; an elongation-only bin cannot establish this mechanism. A distance- or conjunction-branch-resolved analysis is required.

\paragraph{Mars.} The fitted drift is $-17.70$ arcseconds/year, close to the mean-motion prediction, corresponding to about 5.4 degrees over the window. Its detrended RMS is 1.688 degrees and increases from 1.19 to 2.22 by civil century. Geocentric longitude can be particularly sensitive to model errors near opposition. The sighra angular correction itself is zero at exact opposition, so enhanced sensitivity must not be described as a maximum of that correction. Quantitative opposition/conjunction-bin comparisons require a specified additional analysis.

\paragraph{Jupiter and Saturn.} Jupiter's linear-only drift is $+7.84$ arcseconds/year; including the specified 883-year harmonic changes it to $+7.144$. Saturn's linear-only drift is $-29.52$ arcseconds/year, with a precession-corrected offset of $-9.78$ degrees at 2100-01-01 and detrended RMS of 1.401 degrees. Its drift becomes $-27.861$ in the joint fit. The change shows sensitivity to long-period structure; it does not uniquely identify the source of every difference from the approximate mean-motion prediction (Section~\ref{sec:decomp}).

\paragraph{Rahu.} The fitted drift of the node is $+9.34$ arcseconds/year, close to the predicted $+9.5$, and amounts to about 2.8 degrees across the window. The detrended RMS of 1.076 degrees is consistent with the roughly 1.5-degree oscillation of the osculating reference. This statistic therefore compares a mean-node model with an instantaneous osculating node, and it should not be interpreted solely as a periodic error of the Surya Siddhanta. A 17-sample moving average reduces the whole-window RMS from 2.772 to 2.556 degrees. At a ten-day interval, the first and last samples of this average are 160 days apart, or 170 days if the span is taken as the number of samples multiplied by the interval, so it is not an exact 173-day average. The calculation uses valid convolution and excludes incomplete windows at the edges.

\subsection{What this means for the text's users}

A tithi spans 12 degrees of Sun--Moon elongation. The common ayanamsha cancels in the elongation, and the similar solar and lunar drifts largely cancel as well. Across the sampled epochs, the mean absolute elongation error is 1.178 degrees and the maximum is 3.938 degrees. Dividing the mean error by the mean elongation rate of 12.19 degrees/day gives a characteristic timing scale of 2.32 hours. This value is not a measurement of tithi-boundary or eclipse-timing error. Such measurements would require event searches and a varying relative angular speed, and for eclipses also geometric and observer-specific validation.

When tithi indices are computed as $\lfloor \text{elongation}/12 \rfloor$, 3,966 of the 40,176 sampled epochs differ by one tithi between the Surya Siddhanta and the \dT{}-corrected reference, and none differs by more than one on this grid. An angular error below 12 degrees does not guarantee identical tithi indices near a boundary, and ten-day sampling does not establish a bound in continuous time.

\subsection{Frame choice}

Subtracting the scalar frame difference reduces the Sun's whole-window RMS from 4.81 to 3.59 degrees. Reductions are 1.20 for the Moon, 1.08 for Mars, 1.05 for Jupiter, 1.03 for Mercury, 0.98 for Rahu, 0.74 for Saturn and 0.61 for Venus. These results describe the precession-corrected convention defined in Section~\ref{sec:frame-clock}. Comparison in a fixed ecliptic plane would require transforming the full vectors and specifying that plane and its zero direction.

\subsection{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Reading of the text.} A different interpretation of a verse can change a result. We reduce this risk by giving verse references for the relevant calculations and by testing against Mahavir Prasad Srivastava's worked examples. Even so, the implementation remains one of several possible readings.
  \item \textbf{No bija corrections.} These results describe an uncorrected parameter set and are not an upper bound on the errors of historical practice. A correction can improve some epochs or metrics and worsen others.
  \item \textbf{\dT{} model.} The historical polynomials carry epoch-dependent uncertainty, and their continuation into the future is an extrapolation. For scale, a timing change of 1,000 seconds corresponds to about 0.15 degrees of mean lunar motion. This figure is a sensitivity estimate, not a bound on future uncertainty, and we have not assigned a uniform confidence interval to the full 1,100-year window. (NASA \dT{} polynomial specification: \url{https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/LEcat5/deltatpoly.html}.)
  \item \textbf{Future epochs.} Epochs after the period of observations compare two model extrapolations, not recorded historical experience.
  \item \textbf{Osculating node.} Rahu's comparison includes physical oscillations absent from a mean-node model.
  \item \textbf{Sampling and attribution.} The largest and smallest sampled values do not guarantee continuous-time bounds. A detrended residual can contain nonlinear secular structure as well as periodic terms.
  \item \textbf{Numerical implementation.} Historical sine-table interpolation, modern inverse functions and 0.0001-degree output rounding are part of the evaluated implementation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Evaluating this Surya Siddhanta implementation against DE440 from 1550 to 2649~CE yields reproducible longitude residuals for the selected frame and clock conventions. Up to 2249, the Sun has the smallest per-century RMS of the evaluated bodies, rising from 1.14 degrees in the first century to 1.93 degrees in 2150--2249, and its detrended RMS is 0.198 degrees. Saturn's per-century RMS ranges from 4.88 to 20.04 degrees, and Saturn also has the largest linear-only drift ($-29.5$ arcseconds/year). Venus has the largest detrended RMS (7.2 degrees). In absolute value, the precession-corrected offsets at 2100-01-01 exceed the detrended RMS for every body except Venus. This comparison describes the relative scales of the fitted offsets and the remaining structure. It does not constitute a unique physical decomposition.

The results are consistent with contributions from mean-motion errors, limitations of the epicyclic equations, missing lunar perturbations and the trepidation model. The trepidation contribution changes from about $+1.9$ degrees near its reversal in 2299 to $-8.2$ degrees at the final epoch. The 883-year joint fit shows that the estimated drifts of Jupiter and Saturn depend on how long-period structure is treated. The available calculations support the longitude comparison. Claims about individual harmonic amplitudes, historical practice and event timing require further work. The script and data described in Section~\ref{sec:availability} allow the tables and figures to be regenerated from the DE440 kernel.
